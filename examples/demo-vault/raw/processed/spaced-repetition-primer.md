# A Primer on Spaced Repetition

If you review something right after learning it, you waste the review: you still remember it. If you wait too long, you have to relearn it from scratch. Spaced repetition schedules each review for the moment just before you would forget.

The idea goes back to Hermann Ebbinghaus, who measured his own forgetting in the 1880s and found that memory decays quickly at first and then levels off, and that reviews spread out over time beat reviews crammed together.

Modern flashcard software turns this into an algorithm. SuperMemo, written by Piotr Wozniak, introduced the SM-2 algorithm in 1987. Anki, a free and open-source app, uses a variant of it. Each time you answer a card, the app adjusts how long to wait before showing it again: easy cards drift out to months, hard cards come back tomorrow.

The method works best when each card makes you actively retrieve an answer rather than reread it. Reading a card front and back is recognition; producing the answer before flipping is recall, and recall is what strengthens memory.

The catch: spaced repetition only helps you keep what you put in. Badly written cards, or cards about things you never understood, just get memorized as noise.
